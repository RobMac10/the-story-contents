Part One

Before the Ships

Tens of thousands of years with the continent to its own peoples — then, in just thirteen years, a pewter plate on a cliff top and a Dutch ship broken on a reef. The oldest object left by outsiders dates from 1616.

c. 65,000 YEARS AGO

Deep Time — Madjedbebe

Mirarr Country · western Arnhem Land · Chapter: Deep Time

At a rock shelter called Madjedbebe, archaeologists dated grains of buried sand that had last seen sunlight around 65,000 years ago — alongside stone tools and ochre. Not everyone agrees: another team argues for arrival about 50,000–55,000 years ago. The book gives you both findings and leaves the weighing to you.

“Human occupation began around 65,000 years ago…”Nature · 20 July 2017 · Clarkson, Jacobs, Marwick et al.

c. 40,000–42,000 YEARS AGO

Mungo Lady & Mungo Man

Paakantji, Ngiyampaa & Mutthi Mutthi Country · Lake Mungo, NSW · Chapter: Deep Time

In 1968 and 1974, geologist Jim Bowler found two burials in a sand dune beside the dry lake — a woman who had been cremated, and a man laid on his back with red ochre. She was returned to Country in 1992. On 17 November 2017, after about forty years in Canberra, he was driven home in a hearse.

25 OCTOBER 1616

The Plate at Cape Inscription

Malgana Country · Dirk Hartog Island, Shark Bay, WA · Chapter: The Plate at Cape Inscription, 1616

The Dutch ship Eendracht stopped for just two days on its way to Java. On a cliff top the crew nailed a flat pewter plate to a post and scratched their names and the date into it. In 1697 Willem de Vlamingh found it still there, took it home, and left a copy with his own visit added.

“1616. On 25 October the ship Eendracht of Amsterdam arrived here… On the 27th of the same month they set sail for Bantam.”The words on the plate · 1616 · Translation: WA Museum

4 JUNE 1629

Batavia — Wreck & Mutiny

Houtman Abrolhos · off the WA coast · Chapter: Batavia, 1629

The Batavia hit Morning Reef with about 316 people aboard. While commander Francisco Pelsaert sailed for help, Jeronimus Cornelisz and his followers killed about 125 survivors on the islands; a group of soldiers led by Wiebbe Hayes held out until the rescue ship arrived. Two young men, Wouter Loos and Jan Pelgrom de Bye, were left on the mainland — and never heard of again. The killings are named in the book, not described.

1700s — EVERY SEASON

The Macassan Visitors

Arnhem Land coast · Northern Territory · Companion case

Long before British settlement, fleets from Makassar, in Sulawesi, sailed in on the monsoon to fish for trepang (sea cucumber) along the north coast — trading, working alongside Aboriginal people, and sailing home again. A very different kind of visit from the ones that follow: they came, they traded, and they left.

Part Two

The Claim

Secret instructions, a flag raised on an island, a fleet in a harbour it renamed — and the people who were already there: Arabanoo, Bennelong and Barangaroo, Pemulwuy, Bungaree. The part closes with the frontier, 1804 to 1838.

30 JULY 1768 · THEN 29 APRIL 1770

Kamay — The Secret Instructions Meet the Shore

Gweagal & Dharawal Country · Kamay (Botany Bay) · Chapters: From the West Coast to Kamay · Kamay

Before the Endeavour sailed, Cook was given secret orders: take possession with the consent of the natives — or, if the country was uninhabited, by marks and inscriptions. At Kamay, two men stood on the shore and opposed the landing. Cook fired three times. The ship stayed eight days, took forty or fifty spears from the huts — and the beads it left behind were still lying there the next morning. Four of those spears were handed back to the La Perouse Aboriginal Community on 23 April 2024.

“You are also with the Consent of the Natives to take Possession of Convenient Situations in the Country in the Name of the King of Great Britain: Or: if you find the Country uninhabited take Possession for his Majesty…”Admiralty Secret Instructions to Cook · 30 July 1768

22 AUGUST 1770

Possession Island — A Whole Coast, Claimed in an Afternoon

Kaurareg, Gudang Yadhaykenu & Ankamuthi Country · Bedanug / Possession Island · Chapter: Possession Island

From a hilltop, Cook raised the flag and claimed “the whole Eastern coast” for King George III — while his own journal, the same day, recorded “a great number of Smokes, a certain sign that they are inhabited.” Two years earlier the Earl of Morton had reminded the voyagers that the peoples they met were “the natural, and in the strictest sense of the word, the legal possessors” of their lands. Kaurareg elders tell the day differently again — some say Cook never came ashore at all.

“…we have daily seen smokes on every part of the Coast we have lately been upon.”Cook · Endeavour Journal · 22 August 1770

26 JANUARY 1788

Warrane — The Fleet Moves to Sydney Cove

Gadigal Country · Warrane (Sydney Cove) · Chapter: Botany Bay and Warrane

Botany Bay proved too open, too shallow and too short of fresh water. Phillip chose a cove up the harbour with a good spring — Warrane — and on the evening of 26 January the colours were displayed on shore. His instructions told him to “live in amity and kindness” with the people of the country, and to protect his own people first. Both orders are in the book, side by side.

DECEMBER 1788 — MAY 1789

Arabanoo — And the Sickness

Sydney Harbour · Chapter: Arabanoo

So the governor could learn the language, Arabanoo was seized on the beach at Manly Cove and kept with a fetter on his leg. Months later he was naming the coves for his captors — and then, in April 1789, boat crews began finding bodies in every cove. Smallpox. Arabanoo nursed the sick who were brought in, caught the disease, and died on 18 May 1789. Where it came from was argued about then — and historians still disagree.

“All dead! all dead!”Arabanoo · Recorded by David Collins · 1798

1789 — 1813

Bennelong & Barangaroo

Wangal Country & Sydney · Chapter: Bennelong and Barangaroo

Bennelong ate at the governor’s table, travelled to England, and in 1796 wrote the first known letter by an Aboriginal person in English. Barangaroo, his wife, refused the settlement’s terms in her own recorded words. Their chapter is told from both sides of the record — the governor’s letters, and what Bennelong asked for himself.

1790 — 1802

Pemulwuy — Twelve Years of Resistance

Bidjigal Country · Sydney region · Chapter: Pemulwuy

For twelve years Pemulwuy led resistance to the settlement spreading across Bidjigal Country — spearing the governor’s gamekeeper, raiding farms, and surviving being shot. He was shot dead on or just before 2 June 1802. His head was cut off and sent to England. It has never been returned.

1802 — 1803 · THEN 1813

Bungaree Sails Around the Continent — Then the Mountains Are Crossed

Broken Bay to Bathurst · Chapters: Bungaree · and the road west

Bungaree, a Kuring-gai man, sailed all the way around the continent with Matthew Flinders — a go-between whose knowledge the voyagers depended on. A decade later, in May 1813, Blaxland, Lawson and Wentworth followed the ridges across the Blue Mountains — along a landscape Darug and Gundungurra people had travelled for thousands of years. Evans reached Bathurst, Cox built the road, and settlement poured west. The cold case asks who really found the way — and what “opening the interior” opened up.

Part Three

Gold and Who Belongs

Gold pulls the whole world to the diggings — and the diggings sit on someone’s Country. Who could dig, who could stay, who could belong? From Eureka in 1854 to Jandamarra in the Kimberley.

3 DECEMBER 1854

Eureka — Ballarat

Wadawurrung Country · Ballarat, Victoria · Chapter: Eureka

Diggers who paid a monthly licence whether they found gold or not — and mostly could not vote — raised their own flag at Bakery Hill and swore to defend their rights and liberties. Before dawn on Sunday 3 December, soldiers and police stormed their stockade. At least twenty-two diggers and six soldiers died. Thirteen men were tried for high treason — and every jury said the same word. Within a year the licence was gone, replaced by a miner’s right that carried the vote.

“We swear by the Southern Cross to stand truly by each other, and fight to defend our rights and liberties.”The oath at Bakery Hill · 30 Nov 1854 · Carboni, The Eureka Stockade (1855)

1855 — 1857

The Walk from Robe

Guichen Bay, Robe · South Australia → Victoria · Chapter: The Walk from Robe

When Victoria taxed every Chinese passenger £10 and limited ships to one passenger per ten tons, the ships simply landed at Robe, where South Australia had no such law. In 1857 thousands of men came ashore — then walked hundreds of kilometres to the goldfields. Later that year South Australia passed a law of its own, and the ships stopped coming.

30 JUNE 1861

Lambing Flat — “Roll Up”

Burrangong · now Young, NSW · Chapter: Lambing Flat

At Lambing Flat, crowds of white miners attacked the Chinese miners’ camps again and again — the worst attack on 30 June 1861, marching behind a banner painted on a tent flap. Police and soldiers were sent; most of the men tried were let go, and two were gaoled. Then New South Wales passed its own £10 law. The banner survives, in the museum at Young.

1863 — 1886

Coranderrk — “We Will Show the Country”

Kulin Country · Badger Creek, Victoria · Chapter: Coranderrk

About forty Kulin people, led by Simon Wonga and William Barak, walked over the Dandenong Ranges and asked the Governor for land of their own. Coranderrk paid its way — houses, crops, baskets and rugs for sale. When managers and the Board tried to take it back, the people walked to Melbourne again and again with petitions. In 1886 a new law forced many families off the station and split them up. Barak stayed until he died in 1903.

“…we don’t want any Board… only one man, that is Mr. Green… and then we will show to the country that we can work it and make it pay.”William Barak · Evidence to the Coranderrk Inquiry · 1881

1879

Torres Strait — A Line Drawn to New Guinea

Torres Strait · Queensland · Chapter: Torres Strait

The peoples of the Strait had fished, gardened, sailed and traded between its islands for thousands of years. In 1879 the Queensland Parliament simply passed an Act annexing the rest of the islands, all the way to the coast of New Guinea. The border that Act drew still runs close to that coast today.

1894 — 1 APRIL 1897

Jandamarra — Bunuba Country

Bunuba Country · Napier & Oscar Ranges, Kimberley, WA · Chapter: Jandamarra

Jandamarra worked as a police tracker at Lillimooloora — until the constable he served held seventeen Bunuba prisoners, many of them his family. On the night of 31 October 1894 he set them free. For almost three years Bunuba people fought from the ranges, until Jandamarra was shot dead at Tunnel Creek. Today the Bunuba people tell this story themselves.

“…the story of Jandamarra speaks to our strength and resilience as a people.”Bunuba Country · Lillimooloora Station

Part Four

Federation

Six colonies become one nation — on paper, at least. Who had a say in making it, who was counted, and who was left out? The book ends not in 1901, but with the vote, in 1902.

DECEMBER 1894

First, the Vote — South Australia

Adelaide · Epilogue: The Vote · The book tells this last, as an epilogue

The timeline puts this stop before Federation, because that is when it happened — the book saves it for the epilogue, because of what it sets up. South Australian women collected more than eleven thousand signatures, and in December 1894 their Parliament gave women the vote and the right to stand for Parliament. The law did not leave out Aboriginal women. Western Australia followed in 1899 — its law did leave Aboriginal people out, unless they owned land.

1 JANUARY 1901

One Nation — Federation

Centennial Park, Sydney · then Melbourne · Chapter: One Nation

On 1 January 1901 the Commonwealth of Australia began, with Edmund Barton as its first Prime Minister; the first federal Parliament opened in Melbourne on 9 May. But near the end of the new Constitution sat section 127 — the counting rule for the new nation.

“In reckoning the numbers of the people of the Commonwealth… aboriginal natives shall not be counted.”The Constitution · Section 127 · 1900

23 DECEMBER 1901

The Dictation Test

Canberra’s first laws · Chapter: The Dictation Test

One of the new Parliament’s first laws was the Immigration Restriction Act. It never named who it was meant to keep out. Instead, an officer could demand a fifty-word dictation passage — in any European language the officer chose. In 1902 and 1903 the test was given 805 times. Forty-six people passed. After 1909, nobody passed it.

12 JUNE 1902

The Vote — Book One Ends Here

Commonwealth Franchise Act · Epilogue: The Vote

The Commonwealth Franchise Act gave women across Australia the vote for the national Parliament — in the same sections that barred Aboriginal natives of Australia, Asia, Africa and the Pacific (except New Zealand) from the roll. Women could vote. Most Aboriginal women and men could not. The book’s last words: the people whose Country it is were still here. So were the records. The story goes on — into Book Two.

Teacher Note

This page is a Book One timeline for Reopen the Record: Australian Histories. It was rebuilt from a writing draft so that it matches the other companion pages: images stay on the screen and tap to enlarge, there is no audio, and student cards do not carry year levels or case numbers. Book chapter labels (for example “Book One · Ch. 1”) stay, because they point to the book, not to a case number.

A Kakadu rock-art photograph was in the writing draft, as the opening image for Madjedbebe. It has been left out, in line with the series rule against showing rock art. The dating dispute (about 65,000 years, or about 50,000–55,000 years) is unchanged: both findings are on the card, and students are left to weigh them.

The Blue Mountains stop (May 1813 — Blaxland, Lawson and Wentworth) now has a published case in the casebook reader: Crossing the Blue Mountains, from page 70. The four paper-and-flag steps of the British claim (1770–1829) have their own case in the same reader: Britain Claims Australia, from page 37. The two investigations are related in time, but they are not the same file. The Claim story app remains the companion for the landing at Kamay, Possession Island, and the early years of the colony.

Image notes: Lake Mungo shows the dry lake and lunette, not ancestral remains. Each image on the cards now has a credit and licence line. The Eureka image is Raffaello Carboni’s 1855 illustration, not a photograph of the conserved flag.
